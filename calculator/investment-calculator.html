How to Calculate Investment Growth

An investment grows in two ways: the money you put in, and the returns that money earns. In the first year the returns are small, because only your initial investment and a few monthly contributions are working for you. But every year the balance is bigger, so the same return rate produces more money — and the contributions you add each month start earning returns of their own. That is why time matters more than the rate in the early years, and why starting early beats saving more later: the growth curve bends upwards the longer it runs.

Monthly contributions make the effect much stronger, because every contribution starts earning returns in the month it goes in — this calculator adds them at the start of each month, the same annuity-due basis the SIP Calculator uses for monthly-only investing. If you are investing a lump sum with no monthly top-ups, the Compound Interest Calculator shows the same growth as a fixed-rate deposit, and the Retirement Calculator extends this idea to a full career of saving, with inflation taken into account.

Investment Calculator Formula

g = 1 + r ÷ 12  •  each month: balance = (balance + PMT) × g

A = P(1 + i)N + PMT × [((1 + i)N − 1) ÷ i] × (1 + i)

P = Initial investment  •  PMT = Monthly contribution  •  r = Expected annual return ÷ 100  •  i = r ÷ 12 (monthly return)  •  N = 12 × years (number of months)

This calculator simulates month by month. The monthly contribution is added at the start of each month, then the whole balance is multiplied by the monthly growth factor g. Because contributions go in at the start of the month, they earn returns for that full month — the same annuity-due basis as the SIP Calculator.

For monthly compounding, the simulation above is exactly equal to this closed form, where i = r ÷ 12 (monthly return) and N = 12 × years (number of months).

Investment Calculator Example

Worked example: Initial investment 5,000, monthly contribution 300, expected annual return 10%, 15 years: i = 0.10 ÷ 12 = 0.008333, N = 180, (1.008333)180 = 4.4539, A = 5,000 × 4.4539 + 300 × [(4.4539 − 1) ÷ 0.008333] × 1.008333 = 22,270 + 125,377 = 147,647. Total invested = 5,000 + 300 × 180 = 59,000; total returns = 147,647 − 59,000 = 88,647. After the first year the balance is 9,325, and after 5 years it is 31,651.

The same method cross-checks exactly against the SIP Calculator — 0 initial + 10,000 a month at 12% for 10 years gives 2,323,391 — and against the Compound Interest Calculator — 10,000 + 500 a month at 8% for 10 years gives 114,279.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an investment calculator?

An investment calculator estimates how much your money could grow over time. You enter an initial investment, a monthly contribution, an expected annual return and a number of years, and it shows the future value of your investment, how much you put in, and how much of the final value is growth.

How is the future value calculated?

This calculator simulates month by month. Each month your contribution is added at the start of the month, then the balance is multiplied by the monthly growth factor g = 1 + r ÷ 12, where r is the annual return as a decimal. For monthly compounding this matches the closed-form A = P(1+i)^N + PMT × [((1+i)^N − 1) ÷ i] × (1+i), with i = r ÷ 12 and N = 12 × years.

What is the difference between this and a SIP calculator?

They use the same maths, but they answer different questions. A SIP calculator is for monthly-only investing in a market-linked fund, with no initial lump sum. This calculator combines an initial investment with monthly contributions, so use it when you are investing a lump sum and topping it up every month.

What is the difference between this and a compound interest calculator?

Very little in the maths — both grow a lump sum plus monthly contributions by monthly compounding. The difference is the framing: the Compound Interest Calculator is built around a fixed interest rate on a deposit, while this calculator is built around an expected investment return, with the focus on future value, total invested and total returns.

What return rate should I use?

Use a rate that matches the kind of investment you are modelling, and remember it is an assumption, not a promise. Broad stock-market index funds have returned around 10% a year on average over long periods in the US, and around 12% in India, but any single 15-year period can be higher or lower. Try a lower rate too, to see how much the result changes.

Are the returns shown here guaranteed?

No. The future value is an estimate based on the return rate you enter, and real investments go up and down. Markets can fall for years at a time, and no calculator can predict them. Use the result as a plan, not a promise, and check your local tax rules on investment gains.
