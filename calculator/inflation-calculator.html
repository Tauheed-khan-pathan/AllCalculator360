How to Calculate Inflation

Inflation means prices rise over time, so the same item costs more in the future and the same amount of money buys less. The maths is compound growth: each year, the price is multiplied by one plus the inflation rate, so a $100 item at 3% inflation costs $103 after one year, $106.09 after two years, and so on. The reverse calculation shows purchasing power: divide today's amount by the same growth factor to find what it will be worth in future money. That is why even a small rate makes a big difference over decades — 3% a year for 30 years nearly doubles prices.

Inflation works like compound interest in reverse, so the Compound Interest Calculator uses the same growth formula. If you are planning savings, compare the result with the SIP Calculator to see whether your investments are beating inflation.

Inflation Formula

Future cost = amount × (1 + inflation rate)years

Today’s money will be worth = amount ÷ (1 + inflation rate)years

Inflation Example

Here is a worked example using the formulas above.

Worked example: $1,000 at 3% inflation for 10 years: future cost = $1,000 × 1.0310 = $1,000 × 1.34392 = $1,343.92. That is a price increase of $343.92, or 34.39%. The other way round, today’s $1,000 will be worth $1,000 ÷ 1.34392 = $744.09 in 10 years’ money — it loses about a quarter of its buying power. Change the rate or the years above to see how fast the gap grows.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is the future cost calculated?

The future cost is calculated with compound growth: future cost = amount × (1 + inflation rate) raised to the power of the number of years. For example, $1,000 at 3% inflation for 10 years becomes $1,000 × 1.03^10 = $1,343.92. The same compounding works in reverse for purchasing power: today's $1,000 divided by 1.03^10 is worth $744.09 in 10 years' money.

What does "today's money will be worth" mean?

It means purchasing power: how much buying power your money keeps after inflation. If prices rise 3% a year, $1,000 today buys what $1,343.92 will buy in 10 years — so $1,000 saved today will only buy $744.09 worth of today's goods in 10 years. The calculator shows both numbers side by side, so you can see the loss in real terms.

Where do I get the inflation rate to enter?

You enter the rate yourself — this calculator does not fetch live inflation data. Use your country's recent average: for example, the long-run average is roughly 2–3% a year in the US and roughly 5–6% a year in India. Your central bank or national statistics office publishes the official figures. Try a low and a high rate to see the range of possible outcomes.

Is inflation the same every year?

No — actual inflation varies from year to year, and it can spike or fall sharply. This calculator assumes one constant rate for the whole period, which keeps the maths clear but is only an approximation. For planning, it is worth testing a few rates: a low rate, your best guess, and a high rate, so you can see how sensitive the result is.

What inflation rate should I use for long-term planning?

Use the long-run average for your country rather than one year's figure: roughly 2–3% for the US and roughly 5–6% for India are common planning assumptions, but check your own country's history. For retirement planning, many people test a higher rate as a safety margin, because even a 1% difference compounds into a large gap over 20 or 30 years.

Does this calculator use live inflation data?

No. This is a static calculator: the inflation rate is entered by you, and nothing is fetched from the internet. Actual inflation varies over time and differs by country and by what you buy, so the result is an estimate based on the rate you choose, not a prediction. Re-check with current official figures when you need a precise number.
