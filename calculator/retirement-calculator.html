How to Calculate Your Retirement Savings

Retirement planning comes down to one question: how much will your savings be worth when you stop working? The answer depends on what you have saved already, how much you add each month, the return your investments earn, and how many years you have left. Because growth compounds month after month, the number of years matters even more than the monthly amount — starting ten years earlier can more than double the final nest egg.

Just as important is inflation. A large future nest egg buys far less than the same number buys today, so always judge your plan by the inflation-adjusted value — what the money will be worth in today's prices. To see how monthly investing builds wealth on its own, try the SIP Calculator, and for pure compounding without contributions, the Compound Interest Calculator.

Retirement Calculator Formula

Years to retirement = retirement age − current age  •  Months = years × 12

g = 1 + (annual return rate ÷ 100 ÷ 12)  •  each month: balance = (balance + monthly contribution) × g

Years = retirement age − current age  •  g = monthly growth factor  •  PMT = monthly contribution

This calculator simulates month by month, using the same method as the Compound Interest Calculator. The monthly contribution is added at the start of each month, then the whole balance is multiplied by the monthly growth factor g — this is the annuity-due basis, so each contribution earns growth for that full month. The nest egg at retirement is the simulated future value at the end of the last month.

Total contributions = current savings + (monthly contribution × months)

Total growth = nest egg − total contributions

Inflation-adjusted value = nest egg ÷ (1 + inflation rate ÷ 100)years

The inflation-adjusted value is the nest egg expressed in today's money: it divides the future amount by how much prices will have risen over the years to retirement.

Retirement Example

The worked example below applies the formula above to a 35-year plan, from age 30 to 65.

Worked example: Age 30 to 65 is 35 years (420 months), current savings $50,000, monthly contribution $1,000, annual return 8%, inflation 3%: g = 1 + 0.08 ÷ 12 = 1.006667. Simulating month by month, the nest egg at retirement is $3,123,803. Total contributions = $50,000 + $1,000 × 420 = $470,000; total growth = $3,123,803 − $470,000 = $2,653,803. Inflation-adjusted value = $3,123,803 ÷ (1.03)35 = $3,123,803 ÷ 2.8139 = $1,110,148 in today's money. After the first year the balance is $66,683, and after 5 years it is $148,459.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much should I save for retirement?

There is no single number that fits everyone, because it depends on your spending, your retirement age and how long you expect retirement to last. A common starting point is to aim for a nest egg of 25 times your annual spending in retirement — so if you plan to spend $40,000 a year, that is $1,000,000. Try this calculator with different monthly contributions and retirement ages to see what it takes to reach your own target, and start with an amount you can sustain every month.

What is the 4% rule?

The 4% rule is a rough guideline that says you can withdraw 4% of your nest egg in the first year of retirement, then adjust that amount for inflation each year, and the money should last about 30 years. For example, a $1,000,000 nest egg would support about $40,000 of spending in the first year. It is a general rule of thumb, not a guarantee — actual results depend on market returns, inflation and how long you live.

How does inflation change the result?

Inflation shrinks what your money can buy. This calculator shows the inflation-adjusted value of your nest egg — what it would be worth in today's money — by dividing the future nest egg by (1 + inflation rate)^years. For example, $3,123,803 at retirement with 3% inflation over 35 years is worth $1,110,148 in today's money, because prices will have risen by a factor of 2.81 over that time. That is why the inflation-adjusted figure, not the headline nest egg, is the one to judge your plan by.

Does starting early really matter?

Yes — starting early matters more than saving a large amount later, because compound growth does most of the work over long periods. In the worked example below, $1,000 a month from age 30 to 65 grows to $3,123,803, of which $2,653,803 is growth and only $470,000 is money actually paid in. Starting 10 years later, at 40, with the same $50,000 saved and the same $1,000 a month at 8% would leave you with only $1,324,375 at 65 — less than half — even though you paid in only $120,000 less. Time in the market beats timing the market.

What return rate should I assume?

Use a cautious, long-term average rather than the best years you have seen. Many planners use 7–8% a year for a diversified stock-heavy portfolio before inflation, or 4–5% after inflation, but returns are never guaranteed and vary a lot from year to year. Try this calculator at 5%, 8% and 10% to see how sensitive your plan is to the rate — and if you are close to retirement, assume a lower rate, because a market fall hurts more when you have less time to recover.

Can I retire early with this calculator?

Yes — set the retirement age to the age you want to retire at and see what nest egg that gives you. Retiring early means fewer years of contributions and fewer years of compounding, so the nest egg will be smaller and must last longer. Try different retirement ages and monthly contributions to find the combination that gives you the nest egg you need.
