How to Compare Rent vs Buy

Renting looks cheaper at first: the rent is usually lower than the full monthly cost of owning, and there is no down payment. But rent rises every year, while the mortgage payment stays fixed, and every mortgage payment builds equity while the home gains value. So the right question is not which is cheaper this month, but which is cheaper over the time you will actually stay. That is what the break-even year answers.

Enter the home price, down payment, mortgage rate and term, plus your annual tax, insurance and maintenance. Then enter the monthly rent and how fast it grows, the home's expected appreciation, and the return the down payment would earn if you rented and invested it instead. The calculator works out the total cost of each side year by year. To check the mortgage payment on its own, use the Mortgage Calculator, and to see what the down payment could grow to, use the Compound Interest Calculator.

Rent vs Buy Formula

Monthly P&I = P × r × (1 + r)n ÷ ((1 + r)n − 1)

Unrecovered cost in a year = mortgage interest + property tax + insurance + maintenance + opportunity cost of the down payment − home appreciation that year

Total cost of buying = down payment + all payments so far − equity (home value − loan balance) + investment growth given up on the down payment

Total cost of renting = all rent paid so far, with rent growing each year

P = Loan amount (home price − down payment)  •  r = Monthly mortgage rate (annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100)  •  n = Number of monthly payments (loan term in months)

Principal payments are not a cost — they build your equity, so only the interest counts as unrecovered. Property tax, insurance and maintenance are entered as annual amounts and held flat in this estimate. The break-even year is the first year in which the total cost of buying is no higher than the total cost of renting.

Rent vs Buy Example

Here is the worked example from the calculator's default values — a $300,000 home, 20% down, 7% for 30 years, $1,500 a month in rent.

Worked example: Home price $300,000, down payment 20% = $60,000, so the loan amount P = $240,000. Annual rate 7% for 30 years (360 months): r = 0.07 ÷ 12 = 0.0058333, n = 360, P&I = 240,000 × 0.0058333 × (1.0058333)360 ÷ ((1.0058333)360 − 1) = $1,597 per month ($1,596.73 exact). Add property tax $3,600 ÷ 12 = $300, insurance $1,800 ÷ 12 = $150 and maintenance $3,000 ÷ 12 = $250: monthly ownership cost = $2,297 ($2,296.73 exact), against rent of $1,500 a month.

Worked example: In year 1 the unrecovered cost of buying is $16,723 mortgage interest + $3,600 tax + $1,800 insurance + $3,000 maintenance + $3,000 opportunity cost (5% of the $60,000 down payment) − $9,000 appreciation (3% of $300,000) = $19,123, against $18,000 of rent — so renting is cheaper by $1,123 in year 1. By year 4, buying so far costs $74,659 against $75,305 of rent, so buying is cheaper by $646: the break-even year is year 4. Over the full 30 years, buying costs $357,959 against $856,357 of rent — buying saves $498,398.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it better to rent or buy a home?

It depends on how long you stay, the home price compared with the rent, and the mortgage rate. Buying usually costs more in the first years because of the down payment, mortgage interest, tax, insurance and maintenance, but rent rises every year while the mortgage payment stays fixed and the home gains value. This calculator finds the break-even year: stay longer than that and buying costs less overall; move sooner and renting costs less. For the example below, the break-even year is year 4.

How is the break-even year calculated?

For each year, the calculator adds up the total cost of buying so far and the total rent paid so far, then finds the first year where buying becomes cheaper. The total cost of buying is the down payment plus all mortgage, tax, insurance and maintenance payments, minus the equity you own at the end of the year (home value minus remaining loan balance), plus the investment growth you gave up on the down payment. The total cost of renting is simply all the rent paid so far, with rent growing at the rate you entered.

What is the unrecovered cost of buying?

The unrecovered cost is the part of buying that you never get back in a year: mortgage interest, property tax, home insurance and maintenance, plus the opportunity cost of the down payment (the investment return it would have earned), minus the home's appreciation that year. Principal payments are not a cost — they build your equity — and appreciation reduces the unrecovered cost because the home is worth more. In the worked example, year 1 unrecovered cost is $16,723 interest + $3,600 tax + $1,800 insurance + $3,000 maintenance + $3,000 opportunity cost − $9,000 appreciation = $19,123.

What counts as the cost of renting?

Only the rent itself. The renter keeps the down payment invested, so its growth is counted on the buying side as an opportunity cost instead of being added to the renting side — the two sides stay directly comparable. Rent grows each year at the annual rent growth rate you enter, so a rent of $1,500 a month growing at 3% a year costs $18,000 in year 1 and about $18,540 in year 2.

Does a bigger down payment make buying better?

It helps in two ways and hurts in one. A bigger down payment means a smaller loan, so the monthly mortgage payment and the total interest fall; with 20% or more you also usually avoid PMI. But it raises the opportunity cost, because more of your money is tied up in the house instead of invested. Try it above: on a $300,000 home at 7% for 30 years, raising the down payment from 10% to 20% cuts the principal and interest part from about $1,796 a month to about $1,597 a month.

Is this result exact?

No — it is an estimate, and actual costs vary. The result depends on the rates you enter, and real mortgage rates, rents, home prices and investment returns all change over time. The calculator also leaves out closing costs, selling costs, repairs beyond the maintenance figure, and any tax benefits of owning, because those vary too much from place to place. Use it to compare options and find the break-even year, then check the numbers against real local prices and rates before deciding.
