How to Calculate RD Maturity Value

A Recurring Deposit, or RD, is a savings account where you deposit a fixed amount every month for a chosen tenure, and the bank pays you interest on it. Unlike a fixed deposit, where you deposit one lump sum at the start, an RD suits a regular monthly income — you build a lump sum bit by bit, and every monthly deposit starts earning interest from the month it is paid in.

RD interest is compounded quarterly, so the calculation works in quarterly steps even though you deposit monthly. To estimate your maturity value you need the monthly deposit, the annual interest rate and the tenure. If you have a lump sum to invest instead, compare the result with the FD Calculator, and if you want market-linked growth, look at the SIP Calculator.

RD Calculator Formula

i = annual interest rate ÷ 100 ÷ 4 (quarterly rate)  •  n = tenure in years × 4 (quarters)  •  im = (1 + i)1/3 − 1 (monthly rate)

Each month: balance = (balance + monthly deposit) × (1 + im)

RD interest is compounded quarterly. The annual rate is split into a quarterly rate i, and the monthly rate im is the cube root of (1 + i) minus 1 — so three monthly steps compound to exactly one quarter. Your deposit is added at the start of each month, then the whole balance is multiplied by (1 + im), so every deposit earns interest from the month it is paid in.

Maturity value M = P × [((1 + i)n − 1) ÷ (1 − (1 + i)−1/3)]

P = monthly deposit  •  i = quarterly rate  •  n = number of quarters. The month-by-month simulation and this closed form give exactly the same result.

Total deposits = monthly deposit × months

Interest earned = maturity value − total deposits

RD Example

The worked examples below apply the RD formula above to two different monthly deposits, rates and tenures.

Worked example: ₹5,000 a month at 6.5% for 5 years (60 months): i = 6.5 ÷ 100 ÷ 4 = 0.01625, im = (1.01625)1/3 − 1 = 0.005388. Simulating month by month, the maturity value is ₹3,54,954. Total deposits = ₹5,000 × 60 = ₹3,00,000; interest earned = ₹3,54,954 − ₹3,00,000 = ₹54,954. After the first year the balance is ₹62,143, and after 3 years it is ₹1,99,122.

Second example: ₹10,000 a month at 7% for 3 years (36 months) grows to a maturity value of ₹4,01,373 — total deposits ₹3,60,000 and interest earned ₹41,373. After the first year the balance is ₹1,24,621.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an RD?

A Recurring Deposit (RD) is a savings account offered by banks and post offices where you deposit a fixed amount every month for a chosen tenure, and the bank pays you interest on it. At maturity you get back all your deposits plus the interest earned. Unlike a Fixed Deposit (FD), where you deposit one lump sum at the start, an RD lets you save in small monthly instalments — it suits people with a regular monthly income who want to build a lump sum over time.

How is RD interest calculated?

RD interest is calculated with quarterly compounding. The annual rate is divided into a quarterly rate (i = annual rate ÷ 4), and each monthly deposit earns interest from the month it is deposited. This calculator simulates it month by month: the monthly rate is im = (1 + r ÷ 4)^(1/3) − 1, your deposit is added at the start of each month, and the balance is then multiplied by (1 + im). That is exactly the same as the standard RD formula M = P × [((1 + i)^n − 1) ÷ (1 − (1 + i)^(−1/3))], where P is the monthly deposit, i is the quarterly rate and n is the number of quarters.

What is the minimum and maximum tenure of an RD?

Most banks allow RD tenures from 6 months to 10 years. The most common choices are 1, 2, 3 and 5 years. Longer tenures usually earn a slightly higher rate, and senior citizens typically get an extra 0.50% interest. This calculator covers tenures from 1 to 10 years.

Can I withdraw my RD before maturity?

Yes, but banks charge a premature withdrawal penalty — usually 0.50% to 1% less than the rate that applies to the period your money actually stayed deposited, and some banks also charge a small penalty on the interest already paid. You get back your deposits plus the reduced interest. Some banks also allow a loan against your RD instead of closing it, which can work out cheaper than breaking the deposit.

Is the interest on an RD taxable?

Yes. The interest earned on an RD is added to your income and taxed at your income-tax slab rate — there is no special tax rate for RD interest, and unlike some small-savings schemes there is no tax deduction on the deposits. Banks also deduct TDS (tax deducted at source) at 10% if the interest from all your deposits with that bank exceeds ₹40,000 in a financial year (₹50,000 for senior citizens). You can submit Form 15G or 15H to avoid TDS if your total income is below the taxable limit.

RD vs FD — which should I choose?

Choose an RD when you save from a monthly salary and want to build a lump sum gradually — you deposit a little each month instead of needing a large amount upfront. Choose an FD when you already have a lump sum, because the whole amount earns interest from day one. For the same rate and tenure, an FD on the full amount usually matures higher, simply because more money is invested for longer.
