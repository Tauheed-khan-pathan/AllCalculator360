How to Calculate Compound Interest

Compound interest is interest paid on interest. In the first period you earn interest only on your starting money, but from then on each period's interest is calculated on a bigger balance, so growth speeds up over time. That is why time matters more than the rate in the early years, and why starting early beats saving more later: the curve bends upwards the longer it runs.

Monthly contributions make the effect stronger, because every contribution starts earning interest in the month it goes in — this calculator adds them at the start of each month, the same annuity-due basis the SIP Calculator uses for market-linked investing. Compounding frequency matters too, but only a little: monthly compounding beats yearly compounding by a small margin at the same rate. For a single deposit with no contributions, the FD Calculator shows the same growth as a fixed deposit.

Compound Interest Formula

g = (1 + r/n)(n/12)  •  each month: balance = (balance + PMT) × g

A = P(1 + i)N + PMT × [((1 + i)N − 1) ÷ i] × (1 + i)

P = Initial principal  •  PMT = Monthly contribution  •  r = Annual interest rate ÷ 100  •  n = Compounding periods per year (12 monthly, 4 quarterly, 2 half-yearly, 1 yearly, 365 daily)

This calculator simulates month by month. The monthly contribution is added at the start of each month, then the whole balance is multiplied by the monthly growth factor g. Because contributions go in at the start of the month, they earn interest for that full month — the same annuity-due basis as the SIP Calculator.

For monthly compounding (n = 12), the simulation above is exactly equal to this closed form, where i = r ÷ 12 (monthly rate) and N = 12 × years (number of months).

Compound Interest Calculator Example

Worked example: Initial principal 10,000, monthly contribution 500, annual rate 8%, 10 years, monthly compounding: i = 0.08 ÷ 12 = 0.006667, N = 120, A = 10,000 × (1.006667)120 + 500 × [((1.006667)120 − 1) ÷ 0.006667] × 1.006667 = 114,279. Total contributions = 10,000 + 500 × 120 = 70,000; total interest earned = 114,279 − 70,000 = 44,279. After the first year the balance is 17,096, and it reaches 114,279 after 10 years.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is compound interest?

Compound interest is interest earned on both your original money and the interest it has already earned. Each compounding period adds interest to your balance, so the next period's interest is calculated on a larger amount — your money grows faster over time than with simple interest, which only pays interest on the original amount.

How is compound interest calculated?

This calculator simulates month by month. Each month your monthly contribution is added at the start of the month, then the balance is multiplied by the monthly growth factor g = (1 + r/n)^(n/12), where r is the annual rate as a decimal and n is the number of compounding periods per year. For monthly compounding this matches the closed-form A = P(1+i)^N + PMT × [((1+i)^N − 1)/i] × (1+i), with i = r/12 and N = 12 × years.

Monthly vs yearly compounding — which is better?

Monthly compounding is slightly better, because interest is added twelve times a year and starts earning interest sooner. For example, 100,000 at 7% for 5 years grows to 141,763 with monthly compounding and 140,255 with yearly compounding.

What is the difference between compound interest and SIP returns?

A compound interest calculator grows a lump sum plus regular contributions at a fixed interest rate, like a deposit. A SIP calculator estimates the growth of monthly investments in a market-linked fund at an assumed return rate, which is not guaranteed. Use this calculator for fixed-rate growth and the SIP Calculator for market-linked monthly investing.

Is the interest earned taxable?

Yes, in most countries interest earned is taxable income and is added to your total income for the year. In India, for example, banks deduct TDS if the interest in a year crosses the set limit. Tax rules vary by country, so check your local rules.

What is the difference between simple and compound interest?

Simple interest is paid only on your original amount, so it grows by the same amount every year. Compound interest is paid on your original amount plus the interest already earned, so the amount earned grows each period. Over 10 years the difference is large — that is why long-term savings and investments use compounding.
